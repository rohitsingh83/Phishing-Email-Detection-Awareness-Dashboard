🛡️ "How to Spot a Phishing Email" — The 10 Fundamental Signals

Train your instincts to evaluate incoming messages against these 10 industry indicators:

1. Sender Address

Check the domain following '@'. Attackers often use lookalike domains (e.g. support@paypa1.com).

2. Domain Spelling

Watch for typosquatting (substituting '0' for 'o', or 'rn' for 'm').

3. Unexpected Urgency

Demands to act within "2 hours" or "24 hours" are designed to bypass critical thinking.

4. Suspicious Links

Hover to verify. Look out for raw numerical IP addresses or shortened URLs hiding true hosts.

5. Credential Requests

Legitimate organizations never ask you to enter passwords, PINs, or 2FA codes from email links.

6. Unexpected Attachments

Scrutinize double extensions (e.g. Invoice.pdf.exe) and macro files (.docm).

7. Generic Greetings

Salutations like "Dear Customer" or "Dear Member" suggest automated spray-and-pray attacks.

8. Unusual Payment Requests

Demands for gift cards, cryptocurrency, or sudden changes to supplier bank routing numbers.

9. Threatening Language

Threats of account deletion, immediate arrest, or lawsuits are coercion techniques.

10. Context & Plausibility

Ask yourself: Was I expecting this delivery? Did I enter a lottery? Verify out-of-band.

🎓 Bite-Sized Cybersecurity Micro-Lessons

Lesson 1: The Art of Hovering

Attackers frequently format email text to display a benign URL (e.g., https://mybank.com) while the underlying HTML hyperlink (href) actually points to a deceptive server (e.g., http://198.51.100.10/login).

Display Text: https://company-portal.com
Actual Destination: http://auth.security-update.invalid.test/login

Rule: Always pause and hover your mouse cursor over hyperlinks to preview the real destination in the browser's lower-left corner before clicking.

Lesson 2: Display Name vs. Domain Address

Email protocols allow the sender to configure an arbitrary "Display Name" that appears prominently in email clients, while hiding the true sending mailbox.

Display Name: IT Helpdesk Support
Sending Mailbox: helpdesk-update@compromised-host.invalid.test

Rule: Always expand the sender header to inspect the actual domain following the @ symbol.

Lesson 3: Deceptive Double Extensions

Because default Windows configurations hide known file extensions, attackers name malicious binaries like Quarterly_Invoice.pdf.exe. In an unsuspecting inbox, this appears simply as Quarterly_Invoice.pdf with a document icon.

Rule: Never open unexpected executable scripts (.exe, .vbs, .scr, .bat, .js). Configure your operating system to always display file extensions.

Lesson 4: The HTTPS Padlock Myth

A green padlock or https:// merely proves that communication between your browser and the website is encrypted. It does NOT verify who owns the website or whether they are legitimate.

Rule: Treat encryption as data privacy in transit, never as an endorsement of business identity or security.
